Family history

Fred Radavich, 1883–1939

How Фаддей Родзевич of a village near Molodechno became Fred Radavich of Greenfield, Massachusetts, with a stop in Buenos Aires along the way.

For years, all the family knew was that my great-grandfather Fred came from "Vilna." His 1923 naturalization papers said Vilna, Poland, his 1921 declaration said Vil, Russia, and his death certificate just said Russia.11,13 Searches for Radzevich in the Belarusian church books came up empty.

The record turned up once I worked out why. The family surname falls into the book's binding, so the volunteer who indexed it took the father's patronymic for his surname, and it was filed under "Nikolaev," not Radzevich.1 (More on that in How I found Fred.)

Where he came from

Fred was born Фаддей (Faddei) on 10 June 1883 and baptized three days later. The family was Orthodox, and he was baptized at the Staro-Krasnoselye church of the Molodechno deanery, Vileika district, Vilna gubernia.1 Today that area is in Belarus, between Minsk and Vilnius. Between the world wars it was Poland's Wilno Voivodeship, which explains the "Vilna, Poland" on his American papers.

His parents were Мартин Николаевич Родзевич (Martin Rodzevich) of the village of Mozolei and Елена Яковлевна Козел (Elena Kozel) of Ivontsevichi. They married on 1 June 1875 at the Novo-Krasnoselye church, both twenty years old. Fred's godmother was Anna Kozel, almost certainly Elena's sister: the same surname and the same patronymic, Yakovlevna.2,1

Going back further, Martin's patronymic shows his father was Николай (Nikolai). One candidate for Nikolai's father is Иван Родзевич (Ivan), a peasant of Granich, another village of the Krasnoselye township, who died on 15 November 1890 at 80.4 So far nothing connects him to Nikolai beyond the surname, the place and his age, so he stays a candidate.

The spelling moves between Родзевич and Радзевич (Rodzevich and Radzevich) from record to record. In America it became Radavich, and in Buenos Aires a clerk wrote Rodzewitch.9

The Buenos Aires chapter is the part I find most remarkable. Fred and his wife Anna Shupaka spent most of the 1910s on different continents. In 1918, in the middle of the war and occupation, Fred's draft card still gave her address as Vilna. By 1920 she had somehow reached Argentina, and Fred sailed from New York to meet her there. When they arrived together at Ellis Island that January, she could read Russian and Spanish and was about three months pregnant.10

Anna was born on 22 December 1882, in Vileika according to her obituary, or in Krasne according to a 1966 Social Security record. She outlived Fred by nearly 35 years, was still not a citizen in 1950, and died in 1974 at age 91. She is buried next to him in Greenfield.14

Still looking for

  • Fred and Anna's marriage (about 1904). One place it could be is the 1904 marriage register of the Molodechno town church. On the FamilySearch film, entries 14–21 and 28–30 are there, but sheets 62v–64, entries 22–27 (late May to early June), aren't among the filmed pages.15
  • Anna's baptism (December 1882). It isn't among the December entries in either Krasnoe church's 1882 birth book, though one of them may not be complete on film. The Vileika town church book and the Krasne Catholic book for that year aren't online.16
  • Martin's birth (about 1855, from his age at marriage)2 and the family of his father, Nikolai.
  • How Anna got from Vilna to Buenos Aires. Argentina's immigration database has no entry for her under any spelling tried, and the September 1920 arrivals book that lists Fred hasn't been scanned yet.9

Are we related? If your family is Rodzevich, Radzevich, Radzewicz, Rodzewicz or Radavich from the Krasnoe (Krasnoye, Krasnaye) or Molodechno (Maladzyechna) parishes, Kozel from Ivontsevichi, or Shupaka (Shupak) from Krasnoe or Vileika (Vileyka, Wilejka), I'd love to hear from you.
